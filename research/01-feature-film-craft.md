# Brick feature-film craft (research, Oct 17, 2026)

Method: about 27 searches and 55 fetches (search budget ran out, then the fetch proxy rate-limited at the end). Primary sources used: Animal Logic SIGGRAPH papers and project pages, fxguide, VFX Voice, Animation Magazine, AWN, Cartoon Brew, Creative Bloq, ACMag, Epic-partner coverage of Piece by Piece. Marked [U] = unverified or inferred.
Gaps: no frame-rate statement for Piece by Piece or Movie 2; no jitter magnitudes or fingerprint dimensions; no primary source on LEGO Group in-house shorts or Dreamzzz studio; Glimpse 2017 paper PDF blocked by robots.txt; "Creating the Bricks" doc and fxguide video not watched. ## 1. The films and the TV/streaming tiers (fidelity ladder)
- The LEGO Movie (2014): Animal Logic, dirs Phil Lord and Chris Miller, co-dir Chris McKay (animation). Budget $60-65M, $470.8M worldwide (Wikipedia). 250 crew over about 2.5 years, 18,144 person-days excluding art dept/production/DI (IF Magazine); Animal Logic's own page says 350+ staff, 2+ years. About 1,500 shots, about 90 min (ACMag).
- Brief from the directors: it should look like a boy with unlimited funds made a stop-motion film in his basement (The Credits/Alfie Olivier interview). Freckelton: the aim was that viewers be unsure what they were seeing. Miller: any frame should be something you could actually build.
- Stop-motion homage was real: the end credits of film 1 were genuine stop-motion by Stoopid Stoodios (3 animators, 2 months, 3 min; final 12 s moved 150 pieces per frame per animator). LEGO.com claims a fully physical film would take 10 years and 15M pieces.
- The LEGO Batman Movie (2017, dir McKay): darker film-noir palette, first feature rendered entirely in Glimpse. Rob Coleman calls the look "emulated stop-motion" and allowed looser poses than strict minifig rules.
- The LEGO Ninjago Movie (2017, dirs Bean, Fisher, Logan): stop-motion feel kept for characters, but water, fire, sand, plants are simulated as real phenomena, not brick-built. Fans and critics noted the weaker "made of bricks" feel (see section 8).
- The LEGO Movie 2 (2019, dir Mike Mitchell, co-dir Trisha Gum): returned to "everything is LEGO", with new non-LEGO materials (fabric, paper) handled as things a child manipulates.
- Piece by Piece (2024, dir Morgan Neville, animation dir Howard E. Baker, Pure Imagination Studios): about 93 min, $16M budget, 5 to 6 years. Built in Unreal Engine 5 by Zebu Animation Studios (India); Zebu and CDW Studios each animated about one third, Pure Imagination hires the rest (AWN). It uses documentary grammar (three "gears": documentary, cinematic, music), loosens brick rules in music sequences (floating, space-like moments) and keeps imperfection on purpose.
- Fidelity ladder, high to low [U ordering is my synthesis]: (1) Animal Logic features, every pixel brick-built, wear, stepped motion, no blur. (2) Piece by Piece, real-time UE5 with a real-lens camera model, brick-accurate sets, documentary looseness. (3) LEGO TV CG: Ninjago TV was animated by Wil Film (Denmark) for 10 seasons, then WildBrain (Canada) from season 11 (Wikipedia); the Dragons Rising reboot moved to Unreal Engine with a sculpted, painterly look, shaped torsos to catch light, and squash and stretch to make ninjas look fluid (Yahoo-syndicated interview, art dir Daniel Wang). (4) Monkie Kid: Flying Bark, hand-drawn 2D in Toon Boom Harmony, characters more exaggerated and volumetric than LEGO CG (Toon Boom). (5) LEGO Star Wars: Rebuild the Galaxy (Atomic Cartoons, dir Chris Buckley): builds must look buildable with real parts, held-pose antics, pieces placed frame by frame in transformations (AWN, Animation Magazine); stop-motion character is ambiguous in coverage.
- Dreamzzz: 3D TV, produced by Pure Imagination Studios per Wikipedia; animation studio not confirmed [U]. LEGO Group in-house shorts: no sourced detail found [U].

## 2. Animal Logic pipeline: brick library, builds, scale, renderer
- Source of truth: LEGO supplied its brick database with metadata per brick (size, weight, centre of gravity, connection points). LEGO's own CAD ranged from high-density CAD to low-poly game models and was not render-suitable, so Animal Logic remodelled over 2,000 bricks as subdivision-surface "render bricks" (SIGGRAPH 2014). Pieces carried connection data (studs fit tubes; Technic pins fit only Technic holes).
- Not LDraw. Builds were made in LEGO Digital Designer (LDD, .lxf), which the team customised to enforce legal placement and export XML; a Maya importer/exporter gave finer control. LDD is now discontinued (officially early 2022, successor BrickLink Studio, Wikipedia). For reference the open LDraw library lists 17,164 unique shapes/patterned parts as of its 2026-09 update (ldraw.org).
- "ShellBake": LDD models baked into single-mesh shells with hidden inner geometry removed, multiple LODs (inner studs and tubes dropped), GL-friendly meshes and point clouds for FX. All tagged with brick-database indices, so any shell can be restored to its full brick form. Proprietary format called "bobject".
- Library growth: Movie 1 had 2,008 unique brick types across 1,908 set pieces and 5,088 bricks for 183 unique characters (IF Magazine). Batman: 3,180 distinct bricks, 451 characters. Movie 2: 3,433 brick types, 523 characters, Metalbeard 251 bricks. Library was centralised and shared across shows (Batman and Ninjago reused each other's new bricks).
- Counts: Movie 1 total 3,863,484 bricks used vs 15,080,330 to rebuild the whole film by hand (production notes via AV Club; the AV Club's own "12 million" aside does not match the arithmetic). Ninjago: about 12.7M virtual bricks for the island city (841 m2 real-world footprint). Batman: Gotham about 220M bricks, "larger than 6 football fields". Movie 2: most bricks in one shot 161,737,777; most polygons in one shot about 1.25 trillion.
- Batman render stats (fxguide): largest shot about 3 trillion quads; about 13 quadrillion ray casts across the show; 66,000 instances of the Gotham model navigable in real time; 5,000 area lights in the Batcave; 6,550 CPU-years if rendered on one CPU (Animal Logic page).
- Set-building tools: LEGOscape (procedural fill of mesh volumes such as canyons with brick patterns), Brickville (real-time environment building from the brick and shape library), Scenery and Constructor (Batman era, Maya-based assembly, city grid, procedural lot packing tied to building height, room-behind-window placement), Spawn (scatter dirt, moss), Spruce (procedural plants), Weave (cloth), Brick-Tools (animator search for any brick). Planning check: a canyon of bricks looked aliased and noisy, so landforms were redesigned to be brick-friendly.
- Instancing at every level, from single bricks to city blocks, so no render-time LOD was needed (Batman). Rigs: ALF node-based procedural rig system, Python-built; "uber rigs" generate instanced bricks at render time via Glimpse Archives; scene partitions cull what a shot does not need.
- Renderer: Movie 1 used RenderMan (PRMan 17, new instancing) plus Glimpse, started as a ray-trace accelerator ("FrankenGlimpse" hybrid), only a handful of shots fully Glimpse. Batman was the first feature entirely Glimpse (Heckenberg, Emrose, Reid, Balzer, Roille, Liani, SIGGRAPH 2017). Glimpse is a path tracer; interactive in the Maya viewport; stress test of 55 billion effective bricks ran about 12 fps at 1440x810 with an AO integrator (fxguide 2016). Working space linear P3-D60, some shots ACEScg, finish in ACEScc. No deep output. Tooling around it: "Ash" shader graph, Renderboy auto-render system.
- Software stack by film: Movie 1 Maya, XSI, MARI, PRMan, Glimpse. Ninjago: LDD, Maya (model/surface), Softimage XSI (rig/anim/layout), Houdini (FX), Glimpse, Nuke (VFX Voice).

## 3. Deliberate stop-motion homage: timing and motion rules
- Hard rules, Movie 1: minifigs rotate only at neck, arms, wrists, waist, legs; elbows and knees do not bend; no squash and stretch; no motion blur (a first for the studio); arms do not pass through steering wheels; no shrugs or claps (workarounds: turn away from camera, combine arm/torso/head moves); hips separated from torso for splits with the seam hidden from camera.
- Timing, Movie 1: animated on twos and threes (motionpictures.org/The Credits). 3D World quoting Coleman: twos baseline, threes and fours for fast action to add roughness.
- Timing, Batman (Animation Magazine): stepped keys every 2, 3 or 6 frames; in Batman and Robin dialogue bodies on 4s or 6s while mouths on 2s; fast action on ones to avoid a jarring look. This conflicts with Movie 1's "slower for fast action" [U which is right per shot]. fxguide: dolphins on 2s with no blur.
- Timing, Ninjago: characters mostly on 2s, but FX sims on 1s, never 3s or 4s, because flames and splashes jumped and slipped off the mechs (Cartoon Brew). Water had to be retimed and interpolated to sit with 2s animation (VFX Voice).
- Final delivery was presumably 24 fps with poses on twos meaning 12 poses per second [U, not stated].
- Brick blur: instead of motion blur, a strip or cluster of bricks in the character's colours (black or yellow extras in Batman) trails fast movement. It took months to develop for Movie 1 (3D World). Real motion blur was avoided because it would hide cheats that real stop-motion would also expose (Bean).
- Cheating by replacement: Batman's elbows bend by swapping digital brick pieces; extra arms swap in to reach a wheel; Batman's face is a glowing area under the cowl and expressions are cowl swaps. Batman has nine points of movement (fxguide).
- Faces: 2D libraries on 3D heads, vector-curve face shader mimicking layered decal printing at any resolution, phoneme shapes cut and pasted, small bumps and scratches around eyes and mouths, so faces read like printed stickers. Emmet has no eye highlights. Hero characters had personal face libraries; crowds used baked face-pose textures.
- Acting under constraint: McKay asked for visible seams and hands-on posing rather than heavy computer interpretation (Animation Magazine). Gum ("less is more"): small eye and brow moves carry emotion; Minidolls cannot separate feet or rotate wrists, and choreographers had dancers mimic minifig limits. Bean's team ran tests to convince Warner minifigs could emote.
- Ninjago loosened the rules: arm and head bends beyond what real bricks allow (Brick Architect review), but still held to rigid-plastic feel per Bean.
- Piece by Piece: AWN says animators were already skilled in the LEGO stop-motion style; dance was the main limit, with poses drawn from a 24-hour "Happy" video. On-twos choice not documented [U].

## 4. Surface realism and why it mattered
- Three surfacing layers (SIGGRAPH 2014): (a) automatic colour, transparency and plastic type from LDD data; (b) semi-procedural per-brick detail (scratches, wear, decals, texture variation, stud detail, edge wear); (c) model-level grime and oxidation, with PTEX baked occlusion driving grunge and optional painting at assembly level. One shared LEGO shader reads external reference files for texture choice, plastic type and surface properties.
- Brick-level baseline: mould lines, serial numbers, slight warping; then scratches, dents, roughness, embossed print, fingerprints, grime (flickeringmyth, Animal Logic). 2,000+ bricks were individually surfaced (Animal Logic page).
- Assembly jitter: a procedural transform jitter simulates imperfect assembly so highlights and shadows vary (PRMan era). Batman's page: a jitter algorithm makes bricks slightly misaligned with gaps and crooked pieces. Without jitter, assets looked wrong (Creative Bloq). Magnitudes unpublished [U].
- Batman: every brick has a unique ID that drives scratch, thumbprint, dent and edge maps; 81,854 textures generated; baked occlusion-like modulation for grunge and oxidation; brick textures randomised or hand-picked per asset into a "surfacing manifest" (fxguide, Animal Logic, Jebens SIGGRAPH 2017).
- Fingerprints: extensive R&D, mainly on Emmet's glossy plastic (LEGO.com citing Phil Lord in "Creating the Bricks").
- Reference: microscope macros of minifig seams, dirt, grime; LEGOLAND California visit and Billund tech scout (outdoor models with peeling resin coat, so outdoor sets got an oxidised, grungy treatment); photographed childhood toys; bite marks, worn decals, dust. Old West set got dirt and oxidation by default.
- Physical shading: bricks are reflective with variable subsurface scattering (fxguide). Glimpse SSS: Jensen dipole plus single scatter, tuned for backlit bricks.
- Nostalgia details baked in: Benny's cracked helmet strap copies a real 1980s defect from Miller's childhood toy. Fans noticed the visible "play wear" (Brothers Brick).
- Why supervisors said it mattered: "stay true to the medium", "no cheating"; real-colour palette only (50+ colours in Movie 1, 79 in Movie 2); early trailer comments praised "real stop-motion"; the compositing supervisor reported high-fives when audiences could not tell.

## 5. Brick-built effects
- Pipeline (Movie 1): Houdini builds glue-constraint networks from real brick connectivity for rigid-body demolition, so buildings break into irregular connected chunks. Voxel effects (smoke, explosions, water, dust, clouds) were simulated, then replaced with bricks matching size and shape. Directors rejected conventional sims as lacking charm; brief was "no cheating".
- Counts and variety: ocean "thousands" of bricks, explosions hundreds, Western train smoke from individual bricks. Brick FX varied by type, size, shape, colour, speed (flickeringmyth). Clouds referenced Douglas Trumbull cloud tanks. Light rigs (headlights) and lasers auto-instanced.
- Movie 2: Sweet Mayhem's explosions were hand-sculpted in LDD and triggered as procedural replacement animation, frame by frame, not converted from a sim (Theriault, FX supervisor). Dust sparingly, via Spawn, bunched in corners. No physical smoke (Blasset).
- Batman: water as a fluid sim with partial motion blur; smoke as a "digital miniature" effect (references Thunderbirds, Fantastic Mr Fox); dolphins brick-built on 2s.
- Ninjago (counter-example): fire, water, sand simulated as real in Houdini and scaled to minifigs. Sand: over 100M grains; punch "air motes" 50 to 100 grains per hit; flames scaled like a lit deodorant can; mechs shed water quickly because LEGO plastic sheds it. Burn damage shifts brick colours through the closest legal LEGO shade; bricks subdivided and jittered to show damage. Effects sims on 1s.
- Other productions: Rebuild the Galaxy puts bricks in explosions and studs in water splashes (pod-race scene). Piece by Piece shows synaesthesia as saturated colour built from brick pieces, designed by a specialist; ModelFarm is credited with water [U scope].

## 6. Camera language and lighting
- Movie 1: a virtual lens kit with fixed focal lengths. Plaisted's old favourite was 32mm; co-director pushed wider, so 18mm action often became 14 to 16mm; 10mm for a few scenes (ACMag). Wide, squat figures broke normal close-up/mid/wide rules, so framing rules were reinvented.
- Camera rigs: virtual cranes, dollies, Steadicam, vehicle mounts, each with defined shake that is adjustable. Interrogation scene shot almost entirely on Steadicam (reference The Dark Knight). Camera "roughed up" to feel big and heavy (IF). Anti-CG rule: avoid locked single-axis pivots; pivot and mount points modelled for physical feel. All keyframed, no capture.
- DOF in Movie 1 came from real-lens proxies toggled on and off; initially a compositing job (cleanup pain), later baked into render. Comp added DOF, chromatic aberration, lens breathing, bokeh, flares and removed flicker/noise.
- Batman: framed as if shot by a LEGO-sized crew with a tiny 50mm lens, shallow physical DOF; Glimpse does DOF with bidirectional tracing through nonlinear lens models; flares built in Nuke as a separate EXR layer added at DI so they can be dialled independently of the grade; a "lens kit" adds stray light and dirty-lens artefacts.
- Movie 2: Glimpse upgrade moved lens flares and DOF into render. Creative Bloq says look was based on 1970s-80s anamorphic lenses with "cheats" [U, not in ACMag].
- Lighting: rigged to mimic a real LEGO set lit with real fixtures (Animal Logic); physical scale mattered for bounce and falloff. Interior: bounce, fill, rim; outdoor: simpler environment light; a grille brick over a fluorescent in homage to Basic Instinct. Lighting is its own dept and runs after camera and animation sign-off.
- Piece by Piece: 2.39:1, Moonlight-style anamorphic flares and oval bokeh, Super 8 for some scenes, handheld, mostly tights and mediums; archival sequences exported to VHS and re-imported; Unreal path tracer plus Lumen with real geographic sun positions; Sequencer for synaesthetic light. Baker: degraded footage is hard to sell because it can read as bad work.

## 7. Crew, departments and decision-making
- Brick-specific structure (Movie 1): a dedicated brick pipeline team and a "custom brick rendering facility" (Animal Logic). Departments: art, asset/modeling (lead modeler John Paul Molloy), brick surfacing, layout (cinematographer Pablo Plaisted was DoP and layout supervisor in one), animation, FX (Houdini), lighting (own supervisor, own schedule), comp (Julien Leveugle), DI, plus LEGO Group design and asset contributors. Environment and layout teams could move or hide any single brick and add from the library.
- Heads: Aidan Sarsfield (CG supervisor/head of production technology), Grant Freckelton (production designer), Rob Coleman (head of animation), Alfie Olivier (animation supervisor), Max Liani (lighting supervisor on Movie 1 per Creative Bloq, Glimpse principal engineer on Batman per Animal Logic), Daniel Heckenberg (Glimpse renderer lead).
- Batman: about 60 animators (peak 49 at once), main animation Sep 2015 to Dec 2016, reported average of about 6 seconds of finished animation per week; 500+ crew over 9,153 days; roles listed: camera direction Behzad Mansoori-Dara, lighting Craig Welsh, CG sup Damien Gray, FX sup Miles Green (fxguide/Animal Logic).
- Ninjago: about 680 artists, about 40 months; six animation leads each with 8 to 12 animators, work split across Sydney, Vancouver, LA; supervisors for modeling (Bradley Sick), rigging (Josh Murtack), surfacing (Jean-Pascal leBlanc), FX (Miles Green), CG (Greg Jowle); animation dir Matt Everitt; art dir Felicity Coonan.
- Movie 2: Vancouver crew of 316, 6,600 animation days; layout sup Regis Schuller; production designer Patrick Hanenberger; art dir Kristen Anderson; CG sups John Rix and Emmanuel Blasset; FX sup Mark Theriault.
- Decision patterns: directors set a rule ("no cheating", buildable frames, stepped motion), then departments built tools to enforce it (LDD legality, ShellBake, jitter, brick blur). Physical prototypes were tested before approval (Movie 2 Ultrakatty spikes looked fine digitally but could not be held). LEGO Group in Billund shipped prototypes and kept a say. McKay: visible seams over polish. Bean: story not locked until final mix. Neville made the film twice (interview cut as first draft, then re-boarded with animators) and pushed back against animators who wanted conventional structure.
- Tool dev is a first-class dept: Animal Logic built renderer, shader graph, scatter, tree, cloth, set builders in house.

## 8. What is praised, what is criticised, and the tells
- Praised: "looks like real bricks" and "every frame looks like a MOC" (Bricking Around, Brothers Brick); visible play wear and fan-aware details; strict rule adherence (minifigs never bend knees); contrast with LEGO video games' flat uniform look. Brick Architect liked Ninjago's stop-motion pauses that remind you these are toys.
- Criticised: Ninjago Movie moved away from brick-built water and landscape and bent limbs past real limits (Brick Architect, CinemaBlend: lack of emphasis on bricks "arguably defeats the whole purpose"). Batman: frenetic cutting and visual overload (DeepFocus). Piece by Piece: RT 83% (124 reviews), Metacritic 63; EW B- and IndieWire B- praised the playful framing but found some sequences better live-action or the detail shallow.
- Early Piece by Piece animation made Pharrell too cheerful, close to Disney; Neville's repeated note was "stop smiling" (AWN).
- Physical brickfilm tells in community tips: light flicker between frames, camera wobble or shifting set, uniform speed with no ease in/out, no anticipation, accidental double frames, hands in frame, visible supports, weak set design, inconsistent grade between clips, static faces (BrickNerd, Firestar, Wikipedia: common frame rates 12/15/24 fps, four-frame walk cycle).
- Cheap CG brick tells [U synthesis from the above]: clean unmarked plastic; perfect grid alignment with no jitter; bendy limbs or squash and stretch; real motion blur and smooth ones-timing everywhere; bricks that intersect or float; colours outside the real palette; non-brick fluids and fire; weightless gimbal-smooth camera and infinite depth of field; flat or generic face decals with no highlights or print layering; no stud logos or mould lines up close; uniform lighting with no scale cues.
- Premium tells: every visible element could be physically built; per-brick randomised wear seeded by part ID; stepped timing with eased holds; replacement animation for bends; hand-built effects; scale-correct lens (about 50mm at toy scale) with real DOF and lens dirt; rigged camera shake; consistent tiny imperfections.

## 9. Implications for Brick Studio [U, my inference]
- Treat brick legality as a data check: only real part IDs and colours, connectivity-validated, no intersections. Open library: LDraw (17,164 shapes as of 2026-09), unlike Animal Logic's LDD route.
- Seed wear and jitter from part ID plus position hash so a rebuilt scene stays stable. Layered surfacing: base mould lines/serial/warp, per-brick scratches/fingerprints/dents, model grime.
- Default timing twos, mouths twos, bodies fours or sixes in dialogue, effects ones; no motion blur; add brick blur as a geometry effect. Swap-based bends. Face decals as 2D layers.
- Effects: hand-sculpt or sim then replace with legal bricks per frame; keep colour shifts inside legal palette.
- Camera: lens kit with fixed focal lengths, rig-specific shake, physical DOF at toy scale, flare and dirt layers separable at grade.
