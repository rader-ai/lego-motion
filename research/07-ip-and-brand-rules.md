# IP and brand rules for brick-style work (research, Oct 17, 2026)

This is research, not legal advice. Nothing here has been reviewed by a lawyer.

Method and gaps: about 19 web searches and 35 page fetches, then the shared search budget ran out and the fetch proxy rate-limited (HTTP 429). Mostly secondary sources (law firm notes, IPKat, Wikipedia, forums) plus LEGO's own Fair Play page and the LDraw legal page. Not retrieved: EUIPO and USPTO registry entries, US case texts, BrickLink Studio and Mecabricks terms, LEGO Terms of Use, brick-style ad campaigns, Nanoblock, Mega/Mattel detail. Unverified or inferred items are tagged [U].

## 1. Trademarks
- Settled: the LEGO word mark and the red logo are LEGO's core marks. The Fair Play page says the logo must never appear on an unofficial site, and the word may only be used as an adjective in a non-commercial reference (not "LEGOs"). Its footer also lists "Minifigure" as a LEGO trademark and says the Basic Brick is registered in some countries.
- Minifigure, EU: two 3D marks, Nos. 50450 (stud on head) and 50518 (no stud), filed 1 Apr 1996, registered 2000 on acquired distinctiveness (IPKat, WTR, WIPO Lex).
- General Court, 16 Jun 2015, T-395/14 and T-396/14, Best-Lock v OHIM: challenge dismissed. Head, body, arms and legs give the figure a human look and no technical result. The drawings did not show the holes or hooks doing a technical job (Noerr, LTO, RWK Goodman).
- CJEU order, 14 Apr 2016, C-451/15 P, Best-Lock v EUIPO: EUR-Lex lists it as an appeal rejected under Art. 181 of the Rules of Procedure. I could not read the operative text, so the exact grounds are [U]. Wikipedia wrongly says the CJEU upheld the mark in 2015; that was the General Court.
- General Court, 6 Dec 2023, T-297/22 (and T-298/22), BB Services (BlueBrixx/Modbrix) v EUIPO, Lego Juris intervening, EU:T:2023:780: marks stay valid. The Court corrected the Board: the head stud, hand hooks and leg/foot holes count as essential features, and a figure does not lose technical function just because it can be played with unconnected.
- Why it still held: the barrel-shaped head, neck, trapezoidal torso and arm and leg shapes are decorative or imaginative. They are not dictated by the nature of the goods, and a designer could do them many other ways. One such essential feature is enough to save the mark (Lavoix, IPKat, EUIPO case-law note).
- Open point: Art. 7(1)(e)(iii), shapes giving substantial value, was not examined. IPKat flags it as unresolved. The action was inadmissible for the marks' Class 9 and 25 goods, so those classes were never tested.
- After 2023: a Dutch retailer blog (2ttoys.nl) says BB Services lost its appeal to the CJEU and the 3D mark stands. I could not get the case number, date or reasoning [U]. I found no other decision through Oct 2026 [U]; check curia.europa.eu.
- Scope caveat: these rulings decide validity, not how close a rival figure can come before infringing (LTO says the same). The infringement line is untested in what I found [U].
- 2x4 brick, EU: CJEU 14 Sep 2010, C-48/09 P, Lego Juris v OHIM (Mega Brands): not registrable. If all essential features are technical, a shape cannot be a mark, even with acquired distinctiveness, and other possible shapes do not help. The Court pointed to unfair-competition law as a different route (IPKat, The Drum).
- Same line elsewhere: OHIM Grand Board used the old patent as proof of function (WTR); German Federal Court of Justice 2009 refused brick-shape protection [U, via Wikipedia]; Canada Supreme Court, Kirkbi v Ritvik, 2005 SCC 65 (Nov 2005): a purely functional design cannot be a trademark or passing off.
- US: not verified. Wikipedia says Lego sued Best-Lock in D. Conn. in Oct 2011 over the minifigure mark; outcome [U]. General US principle [U]: an expired utility patent is strong evidence a feature is functional (TrafFix, 2001).
- Logo on studs: the studs are generic, the embossed LEGO logo is not. An LDraw forum member noted in 2019 that the logo on studs is LEGO Group copyright. Keep studs blank. Check that no tool library renders logo studs by default [U].

## 2. Patents and design rights
- Danish patent application filed 28 Jan 1958 (LEGO history page). US 3,005,282 issued 24 Oct 1961 (Suiter). US term then was 17 years from grant, so expiry about Oct 1978 [U, inferred]. Canada's last patent expired 1988 (Kirkbi). A Library of Congress post says the patent expired in several countries, including US, Canada and Denmark.
- Generic as a result: the stud-and-tube clutch, the basic brick and plate geometry, the idea of interlocking bricks. Pitch and height ratios (LDraw uses 20 LDU per stud, 24 per brick, 8 per plate) are [U] from general knowledge.
- Design rights are the live risk for physical goods. EU design law excludes purely technical features but exempts parts of a "modular system" (Art. 8(3), Reg. 6/2002).
- General Court T-537/22, Delta Sport v EUIPO (reported 4 Mar 2024): Lego's block design survived on the modular-system exception.
- CJEU 4 Sep 2025, C-211/24, LEGO v Pozitiv Energiaforras (ECLI:EU:C:2025:648): defined the "informed user," and held that few infringing pieces in a big set is not a special reason to withhold remedies (PONS IP, IPKat).
- General Court T-628/24 (reported 19 Jan 2026, paywalled): Lego's 2x1 clip block design was invalid for lack of individual character. So design rights are fought element by element.
- Germany: a 2025 ruling found some BlueBrixx bricks too close to design-protected Lego parts. BlueBrixx is selling off affected sets into summer 2026 and moving to its own newly designed studs (PC Games Hardware, Mar 2026). Details [U].
- For a render-only studio, these mostly bite sellers of physical or printable parts. Pure images carry less exposure, but copying a specific newer element exactly is still a flag [U, inference].

## 3. Copyright
- LEGO says it owns copyright in instruction booklets, publications, and catalog and packaging photos. It does not currently object to limited unaltered extracts for non-commercial commentary (Fair Play).
- Brick shapes: copyright claims have a weak record. LDraw forum members cite Interlego v Tyco and Interlego v Coko as failed LEGO copyright suits [U, unverified]. Cobi (Poland) won in 2001 after showing its bricks were original. China is different: LEGO won against CoCo in 2002 and against the Lepin makers in 2021 (CN 30 million yuan punitive damages), both via Wikipedia [U].
- Films and characters (LEGO Movie, Batman Movie, Ninjago Movie) are Warner Bros and LEGO works. Licensed themes (Star Wars, Marvel and so on) belong to the licensors. I did not fetch sources for this; it is standard practice [U].
- Evidence of how the industry treats licensed IP: LEGO Ideas rejects ideas based on active or announced licenses (since 5 Apr 2017) and bars sale of the idea; BrickLink Studio Gallery sets IP-related designs to Display only and logo designs to Private.
- A character built from generic bricks is still that character. Rendering Darth Vader in bricks is a derivative of the original [U, general principle].
- LEGO's printed minifigure faces and torsos are LEGO's artwork. Make original prints only.
- Brick "look" (plastic sheen, scuffs, shallow depth of field) as a style is not owned by anyone I found [U]. Copying LEGO sets, scenes or characters is the risk.
- LEGO's 2019 takedown wave against 3D-print files used DMCA notices and trademark letters. A LEGO spokesperson said the aim was to protect its trademarks, copyrights and patents (3DPI, 9 Oct 2019; FBTB, 16 Oct 2019).

## 4. LEGO Fair Play policy
- It is undated. The linked brochure file name suggests Nov 2018; the footer shows 2026. It is written for fan websites, and every allowance is non-commercial. It says nothing about brickfilms, fan art, fan creations, videos or user-generated content.
- Allowed: refer to LEGO products on a non-commercial fan site, word mark only, as an adjective, same typeface as the text, not bold or highlighted, with the registered symbol each time.
- Not allowed: the red logo on unofficial sites. "LEGO" inside a domain name or internet address. Using LEGO designs, names or marks in unrelated settings without consent.
- Disclaimer: required wherever the word appears, saying LEGO does not sponsor or endorse the site. A disclaimer does not excuse misuse, including logo use.
- No explicit rule on channel names, product names or titles. By implication, "LEGO" in a company, channel or product name is risky [U]. A German YouTuber ("Held der Steine") got a LEGO legal letter in Jan 2019 over a logo with a stylized brick; the outcome was not reported (Brick Fanatics).
- Commercial use: the policy gives no commercial permission. A NYU law professor (Michael Weinberg, not LEGO) argues that naming LEGO to say a model is compatible is fine even in sales, and that LEGO has no rights in the basic brick shape (3DPI). LEGO did not answer on fair use.
- LEGO's Terms of Use and Intellectual Property Notice are linked but I could not read them [U]. A 2012 forum claim that LEGO requires the word "unofficial" on private material is anecdotal.

## 5. Brickfilm makers and YouTube
- History (Wikipedia, secondary): LEGO moved from opposition to encouragement. It had legal trouble with The Magic Portal in the late 1980s and backed down. It launched Lego Studios in 2000, ran a Star Wars movie contest in 2007 and the Tiny Film Festival in 2016.
- LEGO commissions brickfilmers for promotion (BrotherhoodWorkshop; Wicked trailer 2024; Piece by Piece, Focus Features 2024, brick-style with LEGO animation). Those are authorized works, not precedent for unlicensed commercial work.
- Monetization: I found no official LEGO policy on ad revenue or paid brand films. A 2013 Eurobricks thread said no brickfilmer had monetized yet. Forum posts are anecdotes [U].
- Takedowns: the documented 2019 wave hit 3D-print files, not brickfilm channels. I found no documented takedown of a monetized brickfilm channel [U]. Absence of evidence only.
- Fan shows: in 2017 LEGO told a New Zealand exhibitor it does not interfere with fan-show exhibits (Bartneck); its stance on Lepin copies is separate.
- Paid brand work is the gap. Fair Play's non-commercial framing leaves it unaddressed, so the safe route is original bricks and figures, no LEGO names or marks.

## 6. Tool terms
- LDraw parts library: CC BY 2.0 under the Contributor Agreement. Credit the part authors, or "The LDraw Parts Library" for derivatives. Renders, instructions, books and posters made from it are not derivative works, and the Steering Committee says it will not enforce any copyright over renders (ldraw.org legal info).
- LDraw caveats: model files that only reference parts are fine. Anything that embeds converted library parts (POV-Ray files, meshes) is a derivative needing attribution [U for mesh export, my inference]. The All-In-One Installer is CC BY-ND 3.0 US. LDraw is a trademark of the Jessiman estate. Some third-party libraries differ: LGEO originally said private non-commercial use only (forum).
- BrickLink Studio: closed-source freeware (LDraw wiki). LEGO said in Jan 2022 it replaces Digital Designer. It used the LDraw library and added credit after LDraw.org asked. The Eyesight renderer is built on Cycles.
- Studio Gallery rules: IP designs Display only, LEGO and third-party logos Private, own designs only. A BrickLink forum reply says private use is unrestricted and sharing is bound by IP law. I could not read the Studio EULA or Designer Terms, so commercial use of renders is [U] and needs written confirmation.
- Mecabricks terms: fetch failed; nothing verified [U].
- Rebrickable: the downloads page says the data is free to use for any purpose, attribution is optional ("would be nice"). The page shows 2017 timestamps, so the current wording [U]. API terms (commercial use, rate limits) unread. The data lists official LEGO set names, part numbers and color names, which are LEGO's labels.

## 7. How other brick brands behave
- Mega Bloks (now Mattel [U]): won in Canada (2005) and the EU (2010) on brick functionality. Cobi: sued 1998 to 2001, case dismissed. Best-Lock: 2004 to 2016 across Germany, EU and the US; lost the minifigure invalidity fight.
- BlueBrixx: lost the 2023 invalidity case, reportedly lost at the CJEU, and lost German design cases on bricks in 2025. Lego v Steingemachtes (Düsseldorf, 2022) treated minifigures as figurines, not construction toys [U, one blog].
- BanBao settled in 2011 with new packaging and a new figure design. Tyco's Super Blocks stayed legal in 1987 but Tyco could not use the Lego name or "Lego but cheaper" claims [U, Wikipedia].
- Lepin-type copies: Guangdong court 2021, trademark and unfair competition, 30 million yuan. Pattern: LEGO loses on basic bricks, wins on figures, specific parts, packaging, names and exact copies.
- Not found: Nanoblock, and brick-style ad campaigns by non-LEGO brands [U]. Do not infer clearance from their absence.

## 8. What an original brick system needs
- Figure: do not reuse the minifigure's recognised combination (barrel head with top stud, trapezoid torso, C-hook hands, hip-and-leg block with rear holes). Those are the features the General Court called protectable. Use different head, torso, hand and leg forms and proportions. How different is enough is untested [U].
- No logo on studs, no LEGO-like wordmark, no red oval, no red-white-black brand look. Own house mark and typeface.
- Original names for the figure type (not "minifigure" or "minifig"), parts, sets and themes. Avoid City, Creator, Ninjago, Technic, Duplo, Friends and similar.
- Original color names, not LEGO or BrickLink labels (for example "Bright Red", "Reddish Brown", "Dark Bluish Gray" [U]). Colors themselves are free; the names are labels.
- No official set models, instruction layouts, printed designs, LEGO Movie characters or licensed characters. No "LEGO" in domain, channel, product or ad keywords.
- Clearly generic: plain studs and tubes, 1x1 to 2x4 bricks and plates, grid pitch, the 2x4 brick shape, building with interlocking bricks, the toy-plastic look [U for slopes and arches]. Verify any newer part against design registrations.

## 9. Questions for counsel, ranked by risk
1. Does our figure design clear the EU 3D marks 50450 and 50518, other-country registrations and any figure designs? Is a render, not a toy, "use" for infringement purposes?
2. Can we sell paid brick-style work to clients with original bricks and no LEGO license, given Fair Play is non-commercial? Which words (compatible, brick, LEGO) can we use in titles, ads, tags and SEO?
3. Client requests for licensed characters or their own brand in brick form: whose license is needed, and does a brick rendering change the analysis?
4. Do any tool libraries (LDraw, Studio, Mecabricks) render logo studs or copy newer parts covered by designs or patents? How do we scrub them?
5. Written terms for commercial use of renders from BrickLink Studio, Mecabricks and exported meshes; correct LDraw attribution; any non-CC parts.
6. Trademark clearance for house mark, figure name, part names and color names (EUIPO, USPTO, domain).
7. Which jurisdictions matter (EU, UK, US, China)? US trade dress and functionality law needs its own review; this report has no US case text.
8. Platform exposure: DMCA and counter-notice strategy, monetization, and what happens if LEGO writes to us.
9. Do we want a written freedom-to-operate opinion, or to approach LEGO, before launching marketing?
