# Official brick set design language (research, Oct 17, 2026)

Method: ~8 searches (shared search budget ran out) and ~40 fetches. Primary sources: designer interviews (Brickset, New Elementary, Blocks, Design Week), LDraw LDConfig.ldr (two dated copies), Merlin's Bricks ID table, BrickNerd, brickmissing, LEGO Ideas guidelines, Unofficial LEGO Builder's Guide ch. 6.
Gaps: the fetch proxy began returning 429 mid-run, so I never read box art, catalog photography, instruction booklet or SNOT glossary sources. Sections 4 to 8 therefore mix sourced facts with background knowledge, tagged [U] (unverified or inferred). Not reachable: library.ldraw.org, Rebrickable, BrickLink catalog, Brickimedia, Brothers Brick (robots), BrickNerd tag pages.
Hex caveat: the live ldraw.org LDConfig (updated at least through 2025-07-09) could not be downloaded. Tables below use mirrored copies dated 2022-03-31 and 2016-12-28.

## 1. How official designers work
- Team: a theme typically has about 4 designers (10 to 12 for big themes); one designer usually owns one set; 39 nationalities in the design team; weekly show-and-tell; sets ship in two waves a year (summer, January). Billund has about 120 designers; development takes about 12 months in three stages (market research, design and prototyping, validation with kids, parents, focus groups).
- Flow: paper sketch for function and size, then rough physical sketch model, then iterate between a physical "library" of every element and digital models (LEGO Digital Designer Pro style tools) until the deadline. Physical building stays central because software cannot judge gravity, handling or stability.
- Sketch models are pitches: they may use glue, spray paint, 3D printed parts and "illegal" techniques, and are budget-checked digitally. Final sets must reuse catalogue elements to limit new-mould cost, so many sketch ideas get dropped or changed. Parts can change what a model reads as (a black leaf piece made a Batmobile front read as a leaf).
- Reviews: designers review each other's models; a design manager, a "model coach" and a building instructions specialist join the loop. Near the end, instructions staff and quality staff build through the model and challenge anything hard to show or too hard for the age mark. Kids play-test prototypes; robots test mechanisms; heat exposure safety tests; IP partners (Disney, Lucasfilm) approve designs.
- Constraints: age mark sets brick count and colour accessibility (a 7 to 8 brief keeps counts manageable and colours easy to find). Each theme gets a limited allotment of recolours that themes trade. New mould size is shaped by age marks, similarity to existing elements and commonality (a 1x5 plate became a 2x6 for that reason). Wall thickness is limited to brick, plate or half plate. Graphic designers make prints legible at minifig size; prints cost money, so they are used sparingly and placed for the display angle.
- Part counts seen: 10350 Tudor Corner 3,266 (US$229.99); 10356 Star Trek Enterprise-D 3,600 (US$399.99); 75192 Falcon 7,541 (one designer, about 8 months; weight and stability cap model size); 31215 Sunflowers 2,615; 21062 Trevi 1,880 (2014 predecessor 731); 10331 Kingfisher 834. Ideas rule: 200 to 5,000 pieces.
- Colours per model: no source states a typical count. The Pirates of Barracuda Bay team used only system bricks to keep a retro look. Sunflowers works from three yellows. Kingfisher uses a muted blue/orange scheme with olive, tan and trans accents. Infer small sets use roughly 4 to 8 solid colours [U].
- Abstraction habits: pick one reference version (series season, comic era) and commit; simplify to function (an arcade lever moves a printed tile); put small stories in civilian minifigs and hidden micro-models; exaggerate shadow and texture for distance reading (Trevi uses tan shading under overhangs); interiors are never empty (Tudor Corner has three furnished floors and a removable stair); use a real-world nod (checkered half-timber, coat of arms in a tile mosaic) instead of literal copies. Design the hardest part first (Tudor roof).
- "Aha" rule: each bag should teach one new use of a brick; designers avoid repeating a roof technique across sets (Ninjago Gardens 71741).
- Reuse over printing: a single multi-use element (lanyard, racing helmet, 2x2 logo tile) is preferred to torso prints. Internal ribs stop canopies flexing. Canopies stay removable with two studs so kids can reach figures.

## 2. Palette
- Counts: no official palette published since 2016 (BrickNerd). BrickNerd's unofficial 2023 edition: 44 solids, +3 in 2024 (Reddish Orange, Umber Brown, Sienna Brown) = 47 solids, plus 29 non-solids (trans, glitter, satin, metallic, pearl, chrome, glow). New since 2016: Coral, Neon Yellow, Medium Tan, Medium Brown; Dark Turquoise returned. 2025: 426 White v3 and 427 Cool Yellow v3 (reformulations, first in 76313, Jan 2025). 2026: 430 Warm Pink and 431 Blue Violet (first in Friends Jan 2026 and Botanicals 11508 Daisies). Colours used per year (brickmissing, Rebrickable names): 17 in 1980, 35 in 1994, 72 in 2000, 108 peak in 2004, 62 in 2008, 76 in 2015, 88 in 2024; 193 distinct System colours 1949 to 2026.
- 2003/2004 grey change: old Light Gray and Dark Gray replaced by Light and Dark Bluish Gray; Brown retired by 2006 for Reddish Brown; 2004 rationalised palette (about one in, one out). Minifig heads were yellow until 2003, naturalistic tones arrived with licensed lines 2003 to 2005.
- Why few colours: each colour costs money and similar shades are hard to tell apart in bricks and instructions. BrickNerd argues the solid palette is near a ceiling.
- Families (BrickNerd): greyscale White, Light Bluish Grey, Dark Bluish Grey, Black; sand/low saturation Dark Tan, Olive Green, Sand Green, Sand Blue (Tan sits beside); eight common skin tones (Umber Brown, Sienna Brown, Medium Tan, Medium Brown, Light Nougat, Nougat, Medium Nougat, Reddish Brown).

## 3. Scales
- Stud pitch 8 mm, brick 9.6 mm tall, plate 3.2 mm (3 plates per brick, confirmed in Builder's Guide grid advice) [U for the mm values]. So 125 studs per metre of model. LDraw: 20 LDU per stud, 1 LDU = 0.4 mm [U].
- Minifig: about 40 mm tall (Wikipedia: about 4 cm; Eurobricks 41.6 mm with hair). Ratio is a range: 1:42 by height (brickarchitect), about 1:48 (Builder's Guide), 1:30 to 1:48 "classic" (Cult of LEGO via zoeblade), 1:38 sometimes for vehicles because figures are wide, 1:44 to 1:48 for buildings (Eurobricks tutorial; a standard minifig stands for 1.80 m). Real metres per stud = 8 mm x N: 0.336 m at 1:42, 0.384 m at 1:48. Rule of thumb: about 3 studs per real metre; round dimensions up to the next brick or plate; walls at least 1 brick thick; doorways get tight at 1:48.
- Other figure scales (brickarchitect and Eurobricks): minidoll 1:38 (about 44.8 mm); Miniland 1:17 (about 102 mm); microfig 1:80 (20.8 mm); statue fig 1:125.
- Microscale: one 1x1 round brick or plate stack stands for a person; roughly one brick height per 6 ft (1.8 m); keep silhouette and main features, drop sills, signs, statues; windows by plates or side-stud bricks; designers use microscale sketches to test footprints before full builds (Ninjago Gardens). Midi/mini scale (Speed Champions 8-wide cars, vehicle sets) [U]: use when minifig scale is too big but figures or recognisable features must remain.
- Display/UCS-type: scale is secondary to silhouette, heft and greebling. Trevi stays an interpretation (2 module wide windows set proportions; statues minifig scale). Star Trek set gives no stated scale. Technic and Icons cars use 1:8 to 1:10 style fixed scales [U].
- Mosaics (Art): flat images from 1x1 plates/tiles on baseplates; Sunflowers instead layers plates and tiles to mimic impasto, with three yellows [U on baseplate counts].

## 4. Techniques and vocabulary
- SNOT (studs not on top): sideways building via brackets, headlight bricks, studs-sideways plates. Ninjago Gardens mixes sideways building on lower floors, studs-up on walkways, 45 degree wall on a museum.
- Studs-up vs tiled: studs stay visible by default; Star Trek kept studs so the model is not "too clean and crisp"; tiles used for floors, roads, mosaics (tile coat of arms), screens, water, clocks. Brick Sketches busts use tiles for background variety.
- NPU (nice part usage): unexpected use of a part for a different job (inverted pill and antennae as window trim; leaf pieces on Corinthian pillars; pagoda plates as water ripples; Dark Orange slopes hiding a wing gap).
- Greebling: small surface detail (panel lines, pipes). Falcon uses finer greebling than predecessors; Star Trek keeps it restrained so uniform colour carries the shape.
- Curves and slopes: curved and inverted slopes carry plumage and bows; new cutout curved slopes make stepped bows; wedge plates (2x2 pairs) give faces expression; limits come from plastic thickness and strength.
- Connectors: hinges, clips and bars, lampholders with bars, bar to hollow stud, Technic S-brackets inside bases, Technic frames inside big models (Falcon). Internal structure stays hidden.
- Texture: tile mosaics, half-timber with angled beams, checkered black/white pattern, rippled plates, bare studs for splashes, rock-texture parts catching light.
- Landscaping and baseplates [U]: sets often sit on a baseplate or round base; terrain from layered plates and slopes; plants from 1x1 and leaf elements.
- Forced perspective [U]: used in small vignettes and minibuild backgrounds (shrinking features with distance); common in AFOL scenes.
- Brick built vs printed: printed 1x1 and 2x2 tiles, round tiles and windscreens for signatures, clocks, logos; stickers mainly on large flat areas and large display sets [U]; prints are reused across sets to justify cost.
- Hidden joke detail: micro models of other sets, appliances, furniture inside buildings.

## 5. Box art and catalog imagery (mostly [U])
- Image type: current boxes use CG renders or lightly retouched photos of the built model; lifestyle shots add kids or figures [U]. Hero shot is a three quarter high angle, model large, filling 60 to 80 percent of the front [U].
- Composition [U]: action pose figures in the foreground, set behind, themed backdrop (sky, street, space, jungle) with gentle depth blur; inset circular callouts magnify play features; a lineup of included minifigs is shown on box back or side; display lines (Icons, Architecture, Modulars) use dark or black boxes and calm studio backgrounds with a single hero model.
- Layout [U]: logo block at top left, product name and set number, age mark and piece count in lower corners, theme logo; describe structure only, do not copy marks.
- Lighting [U]: soft key light from upper left, slight rim light, glossy ABS highlights on edges, soft contact shadow; transparent parts tinted with bright internal glow for effects (printed glow on back of a windshield for the deflector dish in Star Trek).
- Display angle matters: prints and details are chosen for the angle the box art shows (Star Trek underside).

## 6. Building instruction visual language
- Sourced: LPub documents the official look it imitates: portrait A4 pages, assembly scale 0.8 to 1.0 so new parts stay visible, parts list shown beside each assembly, sub-assemblies and step groups on light yellow panels, callouts for submodels (not for more than 5 steps), page and step numbers set separately, rotation by explicit angles.
- Sourced: instructions are designed in tandem with the model (virtual models built while instructions are written); instruction specialists suggest alternative parts; a bright brick is dropped into big grey masses so it is easy to select; a dark tan accent was added partly for the instruction team; each bag has an "aha" moment.
- Typical official conventions [U]: white page; large step number bottom or top left; parts-needed box per step with quantity ("2x"); sub-assembly boxes on pale yellow; bag numbers in circles; "x2" repeat panels; 1:1 size ruler callouts for hard-to-judge parts; rotation icons (circular arrow around a small model); magnifier bubbles; thin dark outlines on 3D renders with soft shading; arrows show insertion direction; new parts at full colour with the existing build normal [U]; almost no text, because language must not matter; final page lineup of figures [U].

## 7. Fan (AFOL) language vs official
- Presentation norms [U]: photography on white (light box, cast shadow) or black reflective surface; vignettes and dioramas with environment, forced perspective, atmospheric lighting and post processing; frequent use of rare, retired or metallic parts; illegal techniques (stress, bent plates) hidden by camera angle.
- Official vs fan: official sets must be buildable by the target age, sturdy for play, in-production parts and colours, price targeted, readable at box size. Ideas rules confirm the official bar: no glue, cutting or modified parts; rare, retired, metallic and glow elements discouraged; minifigs must not dominate; 200 to 5,000 pieces; virtual builds in BrickLink Studio are accepted. A designer's advice: think about producing a product, not just a MOC.
- Fan ideas get adapted: a fan mech became Scoop in Ninjago Gardens, resized to scale; fan models are used for inspiration only (Falcon). Brick Sketches busts follow a fixed format (12 wide, 16 tall, faces 10 studs wide, all humanoids get shoulders).

## 8. Rules that make a render read as an official set
1. Palette lock: only colours from section 2, one dominant, one secondary, one accent. No gradients, no custom hex.
2. Grid lock: all parts on the 8 mm stud grid and 3.2 mm plate heights; nothing fractional, nothing intersecting, nothing floating.
3. Parts lock: only parts that exist in the LDraw library in colours that exist; label exotic parts; avoid retired or metallic unless the brief demands it.
4. Visible studs on top surfaces by default; tiles only where a smooth finish is intentional (roads, floors, screens, water).
5. Chunky simplified shapes: detail below one stud is dropped or printed; key features exaggerated [U]; silhouette reads at thumbnail size.
6. Sturdiness cues: offset stacking, walls at least a brick thick, ribs or thick sections where parts would flex; no unsupported spans.
7. Minifig scale first; keep door and window sizes consistent with a figure (about 3 studs per metre).
8. Detail via elements: clips, bars, 1x1 rounds, printed tiles, nice part usage; keep stickers rare.
9. Show a play or display feature: removable roof, hinge, hidden compartment, furnished interior.
10. Soft high key studio light, glossy ABS, subtle edge highlights, soft shadow, shallow depth of field.
11. Small stories: civilian figures, a pet, a hidden micro-model.
12. Common imitation mistakes [U]: off palette or over saturated neon colours; photoreal weathering and grunge; custom smooth geometry with no stud grid; studs too small or too tall; no seams or bevels; mixed scales; floating or impossible joins; too many exotic parts; every detail a sticker; flat lighting with no soft shadow; copied logos or fonts.
